\title{Adaptive Learning versus Static Robustness: Benchmarking Reinforcement
Learning and Neuroevolution for Swarm Control}

\author[iscte]{Gon\c{c}alo Pombo}
\ead{goncalopombo123@gmail.com}
\author[iscte]{Lu\'is Nunes}

\begin{abstract}
The control of robotic swarms is approached by two communities that rarely meet
on a common benchmark: multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), which promises
\emph{online} adaptability, and bio-inspired optimisation, which offers
pre-programmed robustness. This work provides a controlled, paradigm-agnostic
comparison of three controllers --- a neuroevolved graph network with attention
over neighbours, Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) and Soft Actor--Critic (SAC)
--- on the same high-fidelity 3D foraging simulator with six scenarios of
increasing difficulty, under deterministic evaluation and statistical testing.
On task completion, gradient methods dominate: SAC solves all six scenarios and
PPO five of six, failing the U-maze through \emph{reward hacking}, whereas the
evolutionary controller collapses in the bottleneck scenarios. The picture
inverts under zero-shot scalability: only the attention-based controller
transfers from $N=10$ to $N=100$ agents without retraining, since the fixed-size
policies of PPO/SAC are structurally incompatible with $N\neq 20$. The
hypothesis that adaptive intelligence dominates static robustness is therefore
only \emph{partially} confirmed, revealing a central trade-off between
task performance at a fixed swarm size and scalability to unseen sizes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Robotic swarms promise scalable and resilient solutions for tasks such as
search-and-rescue, environmental monitoring and distributed logistics. The
central engineering question is which control paradigm best sustains operability
under uncertainty. Two schools dominate. \emph{Bio-inspired optimisation} (e.g.
evolutionary algorithms, particle swarm optimisation) searches a global
parameter space \emph{offline} for robust, static controllers
\citep{kennedy1995pso}. \emph{Multi-agent reinforcement learning} (MARL) instead
learns adaptive policies \emph{online} through interaction
\citep{huh2023marl}. Despite extensive work on each, direct and rigorous
comparisons across paradigms remain scarce \citep{majid2023deep,
bettini2024benchmarl}: studies typically refine a single algorithm against
variants of its own family, neglecting cross-paradigm baselines.

This paper addresses that gap with an impartial benchmark. We implement a
high-fidelity 3D foraging simulator and evaluate three controllers --- a
neuroevolved graph network with self-attention over neighbours, PPO and SAC ---
across six scenarios, along three axes: task performance ($P_{\text{task}}$),
robustness to agent failures ($R_{\text{robust}}$) and zero-shot scalability
($S_{\text{scale}}$), all supported by non-parametric significance testing. Our
contribution is twofold: (i) a reproducible, paradigm-agnostic evaluation
protocol; and (ii) evidence of a central trade-off, in which the gradient
methods win on task completion at a fixed swarm size while only the graph-based
controller scales to unseen swarm sizes. The full methodology and results are
detailed in the accompanying dissertation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to swarm systems with parameter
sharing to tame the joint-action explosion \citep{huttenrauch2019deep}, and
graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as the key enabler of scalability:
by modelling the swarm as a graph and attending over neighbours, a policy
trained with few agents can be deployed with many \citep{sun2024graph}. On the
bio-inspired side, neuroevolution optimises network weights without gradients
\citep{salimans2017es}, offering stable controllers for static tasks but limited
\emph{online} adaptability \citep{kegeleirs2025towards}. Comparative surveys of
deep RL versus evolution strategies highlight the lack of a common performance
reference across the two families \citep{majid2023deep}, while standardised MARL
benchmarks remain focused within the learning community
\citep{bettini2024benchmarl}. Applications of deep RL to swarm navigation
\citep{iskandar2023navigation} illustrate the type of single-paradigm study that
this work extends with a systematic, statistically grounded comparison against
the bio-inspired alternative.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Simulation environment}
The environment is a 3D continuous foraging task in a circular arena
(radius $15$\,m) with $N=20$ homogeneous agents that must collect food and
return it to a nest. Six scenarios of increasing difficulty are used: an open
\emph{Sandbox}; three navigation mazes (\emph{U-wall}, \emph{Bottleneck},
\emph{Four Rooms}); and two cooperative tasks (\emph{Cooperative Door},
requiring $\geq 3$ agents to open a passage, and \emph{Cooperative Perception},
with a moving target). Each agent observes an egocentric homing compass to the
nest, eight LiDAR rays for local obstacle sensing, and the relative state of
neighbours --- a partially observable setting. To avoid local minima in the
maze scenarios, the progress reward uses a \emph{geodesic} potential computed by
Dijkstra's algorithm over an inflated occupancy grid, acting as
potential-based reward shaping \citep{ng1999policy} that does not alter the
optimal policy and is used as a training signal only, never as an observation.

\subsection{Controllers}
The \emph{evolutionary controller} (GNN) is a compact network that encodes the
egocentric features and applies scaled dot-product self-attention over the
neighbour set \citep{vaswani2017attention}, producing an action invariant to the
number of neighbours. Its weights are optimised by a $(1{+}\lambda)$ evolution
strategy (population $30$, truncation with elitism, Gaussian mutation), with a
fitness that prioritises the task, $J = \bar{f}\cdot 10^4 + 5000\tanh(\bar{R}/5000)$,
where $\bar{f}$ is the mean food collected and $\bar{R}$ the mean shaped reward;
the $\tanh$ term prevents the saturation that would otherwise make selection
blind. The \emph{learning baselines} are PPO \citep{schulman2017ppo} and SAC
\citep{haarnoja2018sac} from Stable-Baselines3, using a shared multilayer
perceptron policy ($[256,256]$) over the dense neighbourhood observation
(parameter sharing). The attention-over-graph mechanism is thus instantiated
only in the evolutionary controller, which is what enables its zero-shot
transfer.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
All controllers are evaluated deterministically over $30$ paired-seed episodes
per scenario. We report $P_{\text{task}}$ (success rate, with success defined as
at least one item collected) and the mean food collected per episode, a metric
comparable across paradigms. Robustness ($R_{\text{robust}}$) injects the
failure of $10\%$ of agents at mid-episode; scalability ($S_{\text{scale}}$)
tests $N\in\{10,20,50,100\}$ without retraining. Pairwise differences are
assessed with the Mann--Whitney $U$ test, complemented by Welch's $t$-test, at
$\alpha = 0.05$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Task performance}
Table~\ref{tab:task} reports the food collected per episode --- the magnitude
metric --- alongside the success rate. We stress the former: a success rate of
$100\%$ under the definition ``at least one item collected'' is a weak signal,
as the table makes clear. The gradient methods dominate the task: SAC collects
substantially in all six scenarios and PPO in five of six, failing only the
U-wall, where it collects \emph{zero} items despite accumulating a large shaped
reward --- a clear case of \emph{reward hacking}, in which the exploration bonus
is exploited without foraging. The evolutionary controller is the weakest by a
wide margin: although it occasionally reaches a non-zero success rate (e.g.\
$97\%$ in Cooperative Perception), it collects only residual amounts
($2.6$ items there, and essentially zero in the three mazes). This gap between a
high success rate and a near-zero yield is the signature of \emph{fitness
exploitation}: the training fitness rises without translating into task
completion. Of the $18$ pairwise comparisons on food collected, $17$ are
significant ($p<0.001$); the only exception is PPO vs.\ SAC in Four Rooms.

\subsection{Zero-shot scalability}
The trade-off emerges under scalability. Only the attention-based controller is
viable for $N\neq 20$: its success rate \emph{increases} with swarm size, from
$15\%$ at $N=10$ to $100\%$ at $N=100$, with no per-capita degradation. The
MLP policies of PPO/SAC have a fixed-size input and are structurally
incompatible with other swarm sizes. Architectural adaptability --- the property
the hypothesis attributes to MARL --- thus manifests in the \emph{representation}
(graph with attention), not in the optimisation algorithm.

\subsection{Robustness}
Under the failure of $10\%$ of agents at mid-episode, all paradigms retain high
performance (94--99\% of collected items for PPO/SAC; 69--96\% for the GNN where
it functions), with no collapse: parameter sharing and local observation confer
functional redundancy across the swarm.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The collapse of the evolutionary controller in the maze scenarios is not a
property of the architecture --- the same graph-with-attention representation
underpins its scalability advantage --- but of the interaction between the
optimisation method and the reward structure. Neuroevolution optimises from a
single scalar fitness per episode, without temporal credit assignment; it never
learns \emph{which} action mattered. When the task reward is sparse, as in the
mazes, almost every genome collects no food, fitness reduces to the (saturated)
shaping term, and the population settles on a \emph{fitness plateau} where
selective pressure vanishes. The same stochastic nature explains the high
variance between independent runs, absent in the gradient methods: without a
gradient pulling every run to a common optimum, each run is a different random
walk over the fitness landscape, and finding the narrow valley of maze solutions
depends largely on the number of generations explored.

Two implications follow. First, the comparison combines two factors ---
optimisation algorithm and network architecture --- so the limitation observed
is one of \emph{sampling efficiency} of the optimiser, not representational
capacity; integrating the attention module into a gradient-trained policy is the
natural next step to isolate the effect. Second, the engineering value is not in
electing a winner but in identifying the inflection point: the choice of
paradigm should follow the dominant axis of demand --- task performance at a
fixed swarm size (MARL) versus scalability to unseen sizes (graph with
attention).

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We presented an impartial benchmark of a neuroevolved attention controller
against PPO and SAC on a 3D foraging swarm, across six scenarios and three axes
of evaluation with statistical support. The hypothesis that adaptive
intelligence dominates static robustness is only partially confirmed: gradient
methods win decisively on task completion at $N=20$, while only the
attention-based controller scales zero-shot to $N=100$. Future work includes a
gradient-trained attention policy to disentangle optimiser from architecture, a
multi-run protocol to consolidate variance estimates, and validation of the
sim-to-real transfer \citep{tobin2017domain}.
